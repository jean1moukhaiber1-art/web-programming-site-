History of the Internet

The history of the Internet is the history of the development of computer networks, packet switching, communication protocols, and interconnected networks.

Introduction

What is the Internet?

The Internet is a global system of interconnected computer networks that communicate using standardized protocols, particularly the Internet Protocol (IP) and the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP). It allows computers and other devices on different networks to exchange data and provide services such as email, file transfer, remote access, and many other applications.

The Internet did not appear as a single invention. It developed gradually through research in computer networking, packet switching, communication protocols, network infrastructure, and the interconnection of independent networks.

One of the most important predecessors of today's Internet was the ARPANET, a research network funded by the Advanced Research Projects Agency of the U.S. Department of Defense. In 1969, ARPANET began operating with four initial nodes. It provided an important experimental environment for connecting computers over long distances and sharing computing resources.

During the 1970s, researchers worked on a way to connect different packet-switched networks. This work led to the development of TCP/IP, a family of communication protocols designed to allow different networks to communicate with one another. On January 1, 1983, ARPANET completed its transition from the older Network Control Protocol (NCP) to TCP/IP.

During the 1980s, networks such as NSFNET helped expand Internet connectivity among universities and research institutions. The Internet then increasingly moved beyond its original research environment and became an international infrastructure. The retirement of the NSFNET backbone in 1995 accompanied the transition toward a more commercial Internet.

It is important to distinguish the Internet from the World Wide Web. The Internet is the underlying network infrastructure and protocols that connect networks and devices. The World Wide Web is a service that operates on top of the Internet. The Web was developed later by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN and introduced technologies such as HTML, HTTP, and URLs.

Since the 1990s, the Internet has expanded from a research and academic network into a global communication infrastructure. Broadband, Wi-Fi, mobile networks, smartphones, cloud computing, content delivery systems, and newer mobile technologies have changed how people access and use Internet services.

According to the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), approximately 6 billion people, or about 74 percent of the world's population, were using the Internet in 2025. At the same time, approximately 2.2 billion people remained offline, showing that global Internet access continues to expand while differences in availability, affordability, quality, and digital skills remain.

Timeline of the Internet

The following timeline presents major milestones in the development of the Internet, from early networking research to modern global connectivity.

1960s — Foundations of Packet-Switched Networking

During the 1960s, researchers investigated new ways of communicating data between computers. A major development was packet switching, in which information is divided into smaller packets that can travel through a network and later be reassembled at the destination.

Research related to packet switching was conducted independently by several groups, including researchers associated with RAND, MIT, and the UK's National Physical Laboratory (NPL). These developments established important technical foundations for later computer networks.

1969 — ARPANET Begins

In 1969, ARPANET became operational with four initial nodes. They were located at UCLA, the Stanford Research Institute (SRI), UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah.

ARPANET became an important experimental network for connecting geographically separated computers and sharing computing resources. It is widely recognized as a major predecessor of the modern Internet.

1970 — Network Control Protocol

The Network Working Group developed the Network Control Protocol (NCP), which became the main host-to-host protocol used by ARPANET during the early 1970s.

1971–1972 — Email and Public Demonstration

Electronic mail became one of the earliest important network applications. In 1971, Ray Tomlinson developed an email system for ARPANET and introduced the use of the @ symbol to separate the user's name from the destination computer.

In 1972, ARPANET was publicly demonstrated at the International Computer Communication Conference. Email quickly became one of the network's most widely used applications.

1973–1974 — Development of TCP/IP Concepts

Researchers Robert Kahn and Vinton Cerf worked on the problem of connecting different packet-switched networks. Their work contributed to the development of the protocols that eventually became known as TCP/IP.

The goal was to create an architecture in which independent networks could communicate without requiring every network to use the same internal technology.

1977 — Internetting Demonstration

TCP/IP technology was demonstrated across different types of networks. These experiments helped demonstrate the feasibility of connecting multiple independent networks into an interconnected system.

1983 — TCP/IP Becomes the ARPANET Standard

January 1, 1983 marked a major milestone. ARPANET completed its transition from NCP to TCP/IP.

TCP/IP provided a standardized method for addressing, transporting, and routing information between different networks. This helped establish the technical foundation of the Internet as a network of interconnected networks.

1983–1984 — Domain Name System

As networks became larger, remembering numerical IP addresses became increasingly difficult. The Domain Name System (DNS) was developed to provide a distributed system for associating human-readable domain names with network addresses.

Paul Mockapetris developed the early DNS specifications in 1983, with the system subsequently standardized through additional RFC documents.

1986 — NSFNET Expands Academic Networking

The U.S. National Science Foundation launched NSFNET in 1986 to connect researchers to supercomputing centers and support academic networking.

NSFNET became an important backbone for the expanding Internet and helped connect universities and research organizations. Its infrastructure and the networks connected to it contributed significantly to the growth of Internet connectivity.

1989–1990 — ARPANET Is Retired

By the end of the 1980s, the Internet had expanded beyond the original ARPANET environment. ARPANET was eventually decommissioned as its functions became part of the much larger network of interconnected networks.

DARPA identifies 1989 as the end of ARPANET, while the Internet Society's historical account states that ARPANET was finally decommissioned in 1990. The important historical point is that the Internet had already developed into a broader system that no longer depended on ARPANET as its central network.

1990s — Commercialization and Global Expansion

During the 1990s, Internet access expanded rapidly beyond research and education. Commercial Internet service providers began offering connectivity to businesses and individual users.

In 1995, NSF decommissioned the NSFNET backbone as commercial Internet infrastructure had become sufficiently developed. This period marked an important transition toward the commercially operated Internet used today.

1990s — Growth of Web-Based Internet Use

During the 1990s, the Internet became much easier for ordinary users to access. Web browsers, search engines, email services, online businesses, and other Internet applications contributed to rapid growth.

The World Wide Web was one of several important applications running over the Internet. It should therefore be understood as a service built on the Internet rather than as the Internet itself.

2000s — Broadband and Wireless Internet

During the 2000s, broadband connections increasingly replaced slower dial-up connections in many markets. Faster connections made it easier to use multimedia, online services, video, communication platforms, and other data-intensive applications.

Wi-Fi and expanding mobile networks also made Internet access less dependent on a fixed desktop computer.

2007–2010s — Smartphones and Mobile Internet

The widespread adoption of smartphones transformed Internet access. Mobile devices combined computing, communication, cameras, applications, and Internet connectivity in a single portable device.

Mobile broadband allowed users to access Internet services from many locations rather than relying exclusively on fixed connections.

2010s — Cloud Computing and Large-Scale Online Services

Cloud computing became an important part of modern Internet infrastructure. Instead of storing and processing everything locally, organizations and users could access computing, storage, databases, and applications through networked data centers.

Content delivery networks and large-scale data centers also helped deliver online services to users around the world.

2020s — 5G, Cloud Services, AI and Continued Connectivity

During the 2020s, Internet infrastructure continued to evolve through faster mobile networks, fiber broadband, cloud computing, edge computing, Internet of Things devices, and artificial intelligence applications.

According to ITU estimates, approximately 6 billion people, representing about 74 percent of the world's population, used the Internet in 2025. ITU also reported that approximately 2.2 billion people remained offline, showing that global connectivity is still developing.

ITU's 2025 data also reported that 5G reached more than half of the world's population, while differences in affordability, quality, infrastructure, and digital skills continued to affect Internet access.

Today — A Global Network of Networks

Today, the Internet is a global network of interconnected networks supporting communication, education, research, commerce, entertainment, cloud services, online collaboration, and many other applications.

The Internet continues to evolve. Its development is no longer based on a single network such as ARPANET, but on cooperation between many networks, organizations, technologies, standards, service providers, data centers, and communication systems.

Three Major Developments and Key Technologies

Several technologies were essential to the development and expansion of the Internet. Three particularly important developments are packet switching, TCP/IP, and DNS.

1. Packet Switching

Packet switching is a method of transmitting digital information by dividing data into smaller units called packets. These packets can travel through a network and are reassembled at their destination.

Packet switching was fundamental to early computer-networking research and to the development of ARPANET. It provided a different approach from traditional circuit-switched communication because network resources could be shared among different transmissions.

Research into packet switching was carried out independently by several groups during the 1960s, including researchers at RAND, MIT, and the UK's National Physical Laboratory.

2. TCP/IP

TCP/IP is a family of communication protocols that provides the fundamental communication architecture of the Internet.

Internet Protocol (IP) is responsible for addressing and forwarding packets between networks. Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) provides reliable, ordered delivery of data between applications when that service is required.

The development of TCP/IP made it possible to connect independent packet-switched networks while allowing those networks to maintain their own internal technologies.

The transition of ARPANET from NCP to TCP/IP on January 1, 1983 was a major milestone in the development of the modern Internet.

3. Domain Name System (DNS)

The Domain Name System, or DNS, provides a distributed naming system for Internet resources. It allows users to use human-readable domain names rather than having to remember numerical IP addresses.

For example, a domain name can be associated with an IP address through DNS. When a user enters a domain name, DNS systems help locate the corresponding network address.

Paul Mockapetris developed the original DNS specifications in 1983. The system was subsequently refined and standardized through later Internet standards.

DNS became particularly important as the Internet expanded from a relatively small research network into a large global network containing many independently operated networks and hosts.
