History of the Internet

The history of the Internet is the history of the development of computer networks, packet switching, communication protocols, and interconnected networks.

Introduction

The Internet did not appear as one single invention. It developed over several decades through research in computer networking, packet switching, communication protocols, and the connection of different networks.

During the 1960s, researchers investigated how computers could communicate through packet-switched networks. These ideas contributed to the development of ARPANET, one of the most important early computer networks.

Later, researchers developed protocols that allowed different networks to communicate with one another. The development and adoption of TCP/IP became an important foundation of the modern Internet.

The World Wide Web appeared much later. It is important to distinguish the Internet from the Web: the Internet is the underlying network infrastructure, while the Web is a system of information and services that operates on top of the Internet.

Internet Timeline

1961 — Packet-Switching Research

Leonard Kleinrock published important research on packet switching. The idea was that information could be divided into smaller packets that could travel through a network and later be reassembled.

Packet switching became an important technical idea for the development of computer networks.

1962 — Licklider's Network Vision

J.C.R. Licklider promoted the idea of interconnected computers that could allow people and computers to communicate and share information.

His ideas helped influence later research into large-scale computer networking.

1965 — Wide-Area Networking Experiment

Researchers conducted an experiment connecting computers over a wide geographical area. This work demonstrated important possibilities and difficulties involved in computer communication over long distances.

1967 — ARPANET Plan

Research supported by ARPA developed into plans for a new packet-switched network. This network would become known as ARPANET.

1968 — Interface Message Processors

Interface Message Processors, known as IMPs, were developed to connect computers to the ARPANET. They played an important role in moving packets between network nodes.

29 October 1969 — First ARPANET Message

The first ARPANET host-to-host connection was demonstrated between UCLA and the Stanford Research Institute.

The first attempted message was "LOGIN", but the system initially transmitted only "LO" before the receiving system crashed.

December 1969 — Four ARPANET Nodes

By the end of 1969, ARPANET had four initial nodes: UCLA, Stanford Research Institute, UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah.

1970 — Network Control Protocol

The Network Working Group completed the initial ARPANET host-to-host protocol known as Network Control Protocol, or NCP.

NCP allowed computers connected to ARPANET to communicate using a common protocol.

1972 — Email and Public ARPANET Demonstration

ARPANET was publicly demonstrated, helping show that packet-switched networking could support useful computer communication.

Electronic mail also became an important application of ARPANET. Ray Tomlinson developed a system for sending email between computers and used the @ symbol to separate the user name from the host name.

1973 — Internetworking Research

Researchers began working on ways to connect different types of packet-switched networks. This research became known as the Internetting project.

The goal was to allow different networks to communicate without requiring every network to use the same internal technology.

1974 — TCP Concepts

Vint Cerf and Robert Kahn published important work describing a protocol for communication between different packet networks.

This work contributed to the development of TCP/IP, which became one of the fundamental protocol suites of the Internet.

1977 — Internetting Demonstration

A major demonstration connected different networks, including ARPANET, packet radio and satellite networks.

The demonstration showed that different networks could communicate using the developing Internet protocols.

1 January 1983 — TCP/IP Becomes the ARPANET Standard

ARPANET officially transitioned from NCP to TCP/IP. This was a major step in the development of the Internet because TCP/IP was designed to support communication between multiple interconnected networks.

1983 — ARPANET and MILNET

The military portion of the network was separated into MILNET, while ARPANET continued to support research activities.

TCP/IP provided an important common protocol for the interconnected networks.

1983 — Domain Name System

The Domain Name System, or DNS, was developed to make it easier to use names for Internet hosts instead of relying only on numerical addresses.

DNS became an important part of how users locate services and computers on the Internet.

1985 — NSFNET Development

The National Science Foundation began developing NSFNET to support communication between research and educational institutions.

NSFNET later became an important part of the Internet's growing backbone infrastructure.

1986 — NSFNET Expands

NSFNET became an important high-speed network connecting research institutions and helping the Internet expand beyond its earlier research and military communities.

1989 — ARPANET Retirement Begins

By the end of the 1980s, the Internet had developed into a much larger collection of interconnected networks. ARPANET was no longer the central network it had once been.

1990 — ARPANET Is Decommissioned

ARPANET was finally decommissioned as the larger Internet infrastructure continued to grow.

This marked the end of the original ARPANET experiment, but the networking technologies developed through it continued to form part of the Internet.

1990s — Internet Expansion

During the 1990s, the Internet expanded rapidly into universities, businesses and eventually homes.

Commercial Internet services became increasingly important, while the World Wide Web made Internet information easier for ordinary users to access.

Major Developments

Packet Switching

Packet switching allowed information to be divided into packets that could travel through a network. This approach became fundamental to the development of modern computer networking.

ARPANET

ARPANET was one of the most important early packet-switched networks. It provided an environment for researchers to develop and test networking technologies.

TCP/IP

TCP/IP provided a way for different networks to communicate. IP is responsible for addressing and forwarding packets, while TCP provides reliable transport services when required.

DNS

DNS introduced a scalable naming system that allowed users to use names instead of remembering numerical network addresses.

NSFNET and Expansion

NSFNET helped connect research and educational institutions and contributed to the growth of the Internet beyond its original research environment.
