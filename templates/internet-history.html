History of the Internet

The Internet is a global network of interconnected networks. Its development took place over many decades through research in computer networking, packet switching, communication protocols, and network infrastructure.

Introduction

The Internet did not begin as one large network. It developed gradually from research projects that explored how computers could communicate and share information across long distances. Several researchers and organizations contributed to this development over many years.

Today, the Internet is used around the world for communication, education, research, business, entertainment, cloud services, online collaboration, and many other activities. However, access is still not equal everywhere, and differences in infrastructure, affordability, quality, and digital skills continue to affect who can fully use Internet services.

Internet Timeline

1960s — The Early Foundations

In the 1960s, researchers began exploring new ways for computers to communicate. One important development was packet switching, an approach in which information is divided into smaller packets before being transmitted.

Researchers at organizations including RAND, MIT, and the UK's National Physical Laboratory contributed to the development and study of packet-switched networking.

These ideas became an important technical foundation for later computer networks.

Source: Internet Society — A Brief History of the Internet

1969 — ARPANET Begins

In 1969, ARPANET became operational with four initial nodes. The first connections linked UCLA, the Stanford Research Institute, UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah.

ARPANET was designed as a research network and became an important experiment in connecting computers over geographically separated locations.

Source: DARPA — ARPANET

1971 — Email Becomes an Important Network Application

Electronic mail became one of the first widely used applications on computer networks. In 1971, Ray Tomlinson developed an email system for ARPANET and introduced the use of the @ symbol to separate a user's name from the destination computer.

Email showed that computer networks could be used not only for sharing computing resources, but also for communication between people.

Source: Internet Society — A Brief History of the Internet

1973–1974 — Internetworking and TCP

As different packet-switched networks were being developed, researchers began working on ways to connect independent networks.

Robert Kahn and Vinton Cerf worked on communication protocols that could allow different networks to exchange information. This work became an important part of the development of TCP/IP.

Source: Internet Society — A Brief History of the Internet

1977 — Demonstrating an Internet of Networks

In 1977, researchers demonstrated communication between different packet-switched networks using the emerging TCP technology.

The experiments helped show that networks using different technologies could be connected through a common communication architecture.

Source: Internet Society — A Brief History of the Internet

1983 — TCP/IP Becomes the Standard

January 1, 1983 was a major milestone in Internet history. ARPANET officially switched from the older Network Control Protocol (NCP) to TCP/IP.

TCP/IP allowed different networks to communicate using a common set of protocols. This helped establish the technical foundation for the Internet as a network of interconnected networks.

Source: DARPA — ARPANET

1983 — The Domain Name System

As the number of computers connected to networks increased, using numerical addresses became increasingly difficult for people.

Paul Mockapetris developed the Domain Name System (DNS), which provided a distributed system for associating human-readable domain names with numerical network addresses.

DNS became an important part of the Internet because it allowed users to work with names instead of having to remember numerical IP addresses.

Source: Internet Society — A Brief History of the Internet

1986 — NSFNET Expands Research Networking

In 1986, the U.S. National Science Foundation established NSFNET to connect researchers and supercomputing centers.

NSFNET became an important part of the growing Internet infrastructure and helped connect universities and research organizations.

Source: National Science Foundation — Birth of the Commercial Internet

1989–1990 — The End of ARPANET

By the end of the 1980s, the Internet had grown beyond the original ARPANET. Other networks had become part of the larger system of interconnected networks.

DARPA identifies 1989 as the end of ARPANET, while the Internet Society describes the network as being decommissioned in 1990. The important change was that the Internet was no longer dependent on ARPANET as its main network.

Sources: DARPA | Internet Society

1990s — The Internet Becomes Commercial

During the 1990s, Internet access expanded beyond research institutions. Commercial Internet service providers began offering connections to businesses and individual users.

The retirement of the NSFNET backbone in 1995 was an important part of the transition toward a commercially operated Internet.

Source: National Science Foundation

2000s — Broadband and Wi-Fi

During the 2000s, broadband Internet connections became increasingly common in many countries. Broadband provided faster access than traditional dial-up connections and made services such as video, online gaming, and multimedia more practical.

Wi-Fi also made it easier for computers and other devices to connect to the Internet without a physical network cable.

Source: International Telecommunication Union

2007–2010s — Smartphones and Mobile Internet

The rapid growth of smartphones changed how people used the Internet. Instead of accessing online services mainly through desktop computers, users could connect through mobile devices almost anywhere with network coverage.

Mobile broadband became an increasingly important part of Internet access, while applications and cloud services became central to everyday online activities.

Source: International Telecommunication Union

2010s — Cloud Computing and Large-Scale Services

Cloud computing became an important part of modern Internet infrastructure. Instead of relying entirely on local computers, users and organizations could access storage, computing resources, databases, and applications through Internet-connected data centers.

Content delivery networks also became important for delivering websites, videos, applications, and other online content to users in different parts of the world.

Source: National Institute of Standards and Technology — The NIST Definition of Cloud Computing

2020s — Faster and More Connected Networks

During the 2020s, Internet infrastructure continued to develop through fiber networks, 5G mobile networks, cloud computing, edge computing, and the Internet of Things.

The COVID-19 pandemic also highlighted how important Internet connectivity had become for education, work, communication, healthcare, and access to services.

Source: ITU — Facts and Figures 2025

2025–Today — A Global Network

Today, the Internet connects billions of people and a very large number of computers, smartphones, servers, sensors, and other devices.

According to the International Telecommunication Union, around 6 billion people were using the Internet in 2025, representing approximately 74 percent of the world's population.

At the same time, about 2.2 billion people remained offline. This means that the development of the Internet is not only about making networks faster, but also about improving availability, affordability, quality, and digital skills.

Modern Internet development also includes technologies such as 5G, IPv6, cloud computing, edge computing, Internet of Things devices, content delivery networks, and increasingly distributed online services.

Source: International Telecommunication Union — Facts and Figures 2025

Key Technologies

Packet Switching

Packet switching is a method of sending data by dividing information into smaller packets. Each packet contains information that helps the network deliver it to its destination.

Packet switching was an important foundation for ARPANET and later Internet networks because it allowed network resources to be shared between different communications.

Source: Internet Society

TCP/IP

TCP/IP is a family of communication protocols that allows different networks and devices to exchange information.

IP provides addressing and routing between networks, while TCP provides reliable and ordered delivery of data when applications require it.

The adoption of TCP/IP by ARPANET in 1983 was an important step toward the Internet architecture used today.

Source: Internet Society

Domain Name System (DNS)

The Domain Name System allows people to use names such as example.com instead of having to remember numerical IP addresses.

DNS uses a distributed system of servers and records to help translate domain names into the network addresses needed to reach Internet resources.

DNS became increasingly important as the number of Internet users, computers, networks, and services grew.

Source: Internet Society
